The chapter in 60 seconds

A new line starts as a corridor on a map and ends as a set of pegged tower sites. The route is a compromise between cost, landscape, birds, land use and permits, so several options are carried forward and discussed early. An environmental impact assessment and public consultation shape the result, which can take 1 to 10 years. Survey then turns the route into a longitudinal profile. Tower spotting places towers on that profile at lowest cost while keeping every clearance, and computers now search the alternatives that a template cannot.

Why it matters

Compensation alone can reach 20 % of investment, and a lost appeal can restart the permit process. Choices made here fix tower numbers, foundation cost and public acceptance for the life of the line.

15.1Starting point and preliminary workp. 573–575

Network analysis fixes the end points, voltage and conductor section. The designer normally reuses an existing tower family, although new supports matched to the project can save money.

From request to pegged line

Agree angle points with land owners first, then suspension sites, because those can still slide along a straight section. Record birds early, since nesting and migration can only be observed in their seasons, and late data delays the permit.

15.2.1Route principles and alternativesp. 575–578

Dense regions force compromises. Rural lines can be truly optimised: straight route, one tower family, best spotting. Either way, avoid angle points, exposed rock noses, weak soil, polluted sites such as cement plants, air corridors and small crossing angles.

15.2.2Permits and compensationp. 578–580

Approval limits range from 1 to 345 kV, typically above 20 kV. EU Directive 97/11 requires an EIA for lines of at least 220 kV and over 15 km. Germany follows this and may require one for 110 kV or shorter lines after a preliminary check. Normal maintenance needs no permit, but major changes such as uprating may.

Who deals with what

Land owners are paid for right-of-way and sites, usually a lump sum, up to 20 % of line investment in total. Environmental compensation comes on top: first look for reforesting or bird areas near the line, otherwise distant areas or payments. Belgium, for example, uses 5 % of investment. Compensation scales rest on expert judgement, since no scientific basis exists.

15.2.3Environmental impact assessmentp. 580–583

An EIA is the study plus public consultation. It compares the environment with and without the line, then lists measures to avoid, reduce or compensate. The comparison case, the reference alternative, must include how the area would develop anyway. Impacts cover a temporary construction phase and at least 50 years of operation.

What is recorded

The scale decides the answer

Corridor width has no general answer. It depends on how far a viewer would be annoyed: some hundred metres each side on farmland, considerably wider in tourist and recreation areas.

15.2.4Visual impactp. 583–590

First decide whether the landscape can absorb the line. Integrate it, hidden in landscape features, or assert it as a visible element. Scale decides: open plains have great scale and suit tall towers with long spans, while varied, enclosed landscape suits the lowest tower. Routing is the most important single factor.

Qualitative methods suit the public. Quantitative scoring suits in-house route checks, but no formula has won acceptance. Components help too: compact lines, poles in the foreground and lattice at a distance, composite crossarms. Treated conductors can reflect under half of new aluminium. Colour coating can backfire if chosen badly.

Power corridors bundle lines with others or with transport routes, which spares untouched land. Parallel lines should share spans and tower shapes, and avoid semi-parallel routes. A vertical phase arrangement has the narrowest right-of-way but a fence effect. A flat one has the opposite effects.

15.2.5–7People, ecology and land usep. 590–595

Near homes, fields, noise and radio interference must obey limits. Keep lines at the edge of settlement, and bundle with road or rail where possible.

Birds are the main ecological issue. Collision affects all lines, while electrocution mostly affects lines below about 60 kV. Earth wires are particularly likely to cause collisions. Careful routing is among the best and cheapest protections.

Reducing bird collisions

Measures and crossings

Forest crossings go over the trees, allowing for final tree height, or through an aisle. The choice is agreed in advance with owners and authorities. Clearing can even help biodiversity, and mechanical control is preferred to herbicides.

15.4.2Tower spottingp. 605–610

Angle towers are fixed, so spotting chooses suspension sites and all heights. By hand, a sagging template for the ruling span and maximum temperature slides over the profile. Its edge is the ground clearance curve, and where that touches the ground the conductor curve is set. Start at fixed points such as crossings. Then check wind span, weight span and their ratio.

15.5Data processingp. 611–617

Program systems pass field data to a common data base, from which profile, spotting, crossings, tower lists and sag tables follow. A GIS adds exact calculation, logical objects in layers and links to project data, which is more than CAD gives. The biggest gain comes later: enter one change once, and every document is revised.

Where the book disagrees with itself

  • Eq. 15.3, p. 597. L = c + s·m·sin z + F adds the Earth curvature term to a horizontal distance, although F is a height correction. Eq. 15.2 and 15.3 also apply c outside the angle term, where (c + s·m)·cos z and sin z would be expected. The calculator shows L both ways. The difference is small at normal distances.
  • Zinc life. P. 590 gives 10 to 30 years. Chapter 12 assumes about 10 to 15 years.
  • Direct or indirect survey, p. 599. Airborne survey is called relatively low cost, then direct systems are called superior on accuracy and cost.
  • Emissions and routing, p. 590. Emissions "need to be considered during route selection", then are "not an aspect of line route selection". Read together: routing keeps distance, and limits are met by design.
