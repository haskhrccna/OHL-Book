The chapter in 60 seconds

Lightning causes about 65 % of line outages. A stroke can reach a line in three ways: nearby ground, inducing a voltage; a phase conductor, through a shielding failure; or the earth wire or tower, causing a back-flashover. Earth wires placed at the right angle intercept the leader, and good tower earthing lets the current drain away. Together they bring outages down to about the book's guideline of 1 to 5 per 100 km and year.

Why it matters

Each flashover is an outage risk and a voltage dip for sensitive plant. Earth wire geometry costs little at design stage, while tower earthing can be the deciding factor once a line runs through poor soil.

4.1Significance of lightningp. 100

International statistics attribute about 65 % of line outages to lightning. Faults produce voltage dips lasting a fraction of a second to about one second, which harm computers and electronics. Aluminium and steel mills and oil plants are sensitive even to short interruptions followed by quick reclosing.

Leaders from the cloud give downward discharges, common over flat land. Leaders from the earth give upward discharges, common on sharp, high objects. A leader's charge relates to the current at about 15 kA per coulomb, which leads to the strike distance.

4.3Frequency and intensityp. 103–106

The keraunic level Nk is the average number of thunderstorm days a year. It is simple but subjective, so the flash density Ng per km² and year is better from an engineering standpoint. Central Europe has 15 to 30 days, the equatorial region of South America up to 200.

4.4Earth wiresp. 106–110

The electrogeometric model draws circles of strike distance around earth wire and phase, and a line at βs·D above ground. A leader arriving on the arc around the phase, between the earth wire circle and the ground line, strikes the phase. Shielding is complete when those three meet at one point. βs is 0.8 for range I, 0.67 for range II.

Surge arresters across insulators can replace earth wires. They have been used up to 145 kV, and remain under consideration for EHV, mostly for economic reasons. Lightning usually governs insulation in range I. It may even govern in range II up to 525 kV with high keraunic levels or poor soil, if switching surges are limited. Monte Carlo and the IEEE FLASH program assess performance.

4.5Earthing for lightningp. 110–112

Tower footing resistance governs back-flashovers. It varies with soil, current and time. Tower surge impedances run from 10 to 30 Ω. Earth surge impedance is 0.25 to 1.0 times the power frequency resistance, so a measured value is a pessimistic estimate. Resistance falls with rising current, until the soil dries out.

A counterpoise starts near 150 Ω and decays to its final resistance. The time is that of the first reflection, travelling at 100 m/μs. Several shorter radial strips decay faster than one long strip.

Where the book disagrees with itself

  • Range I limit, p. 110. "Range I (up to 300 kV)" here, against Chapter 2's Us of 245 kV.
  • 130 kA strokes, p. 109. The text says 1 % of strokes reach 130 kA, then gives 0.6 to 1.2 per 100 km and year from 30 to 60 flashes, which would be 2 %.
  • Circle size, p. 106. The text calls it a "circle of diameter D", but the model needs D as the radius, the strike distance.
  • Shielding count, p. 108. 1/3 of 4 to 8 gives 1.3 to 2.7, and the book prints 1.5 to 2.5.
  • Clause reference, p. 106. Surge impedance is clause 2.1.1, not 2.1.2 as cited.
