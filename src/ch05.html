The chapter in 60 seconds

Earthing protects people first, then equipment and performance. When fault current enters the ground at a tower, the ground surface rises in potential, and that rise appears as touch and step voltages. Earth wires carry much of the current away, and a design check compares the rise with the permissible touch voltage. Resistance follows soil resistivity, which is measured with the Wenner method and usually modelled in two layers.

Why it matters

Touch voltage safety is the only requirement here that comes before everything else. It also feeds lightning performance from Chapter 4. Most of an electrode's resistance sits close to it, so a few metres of counterpoise and a good soil survey decide the outcome.

5.1Purpose of earthingp. 117

EN 50341-1 sets five requirements: safety of persons (priority, and including animals), no damage to third parties, acceptable performance, rating for the maximum earth current, and sufficient mechanical strength and corrosion resistance.

Three types exist: operational, protection and lightning protection earthing. Requirements depend on neutral design, support type and material, and whether the site is exposed, such as bathing areas, camp sites or playgrounds.

5.2Definitions and basicsp. 118

Ground potential around an electrode

Fault current splits between earth and earth wires. The impedance to earth ZE is tower resistance RT in parallel with the iterative network impedance Z∞ of each line direction, which is usually only a few ohms (Fig. 5.4). The reduction factor rE says how much returns through earth.

5.4Earthing for personal safetyp. 122–124

In isolated or resonant systems, usually up to 110 kV, faults can last hours and the touch voltage may not exceed 65 V. This is met if ZE ≤ 130/IE (5.2), where IE = rE·IF. With automatic disconnection within 0.5 s in low-resistance systems, no special limits apply unless the site is sensitive. At 110 kV and above an expectation factor of 0.7 can also apply.

Fixes for sensitive sites: non-conductive barriers or bushes, gravel or asphalt, and grading electrodes. For supports with disconnectors or transformers, an earthing voltage under 250 V and a ring about 1 m out and under 0.5 m deep meets the 65 V limit.

5.10Measuring soil resistivityp. 130–132

The Wenner method uses four rods equally spaced by a. Current flows between the outer pair and voltage is read between the inner pair. Five spacings of 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32 m are usual, as wider spacing reaches deeper. With probe depth much smaller than a, ρE = 2π·a·RE (5.30).

Layers are usually reduced to two: thickness d₁ with ρ₁ over infinite ρ₂. The reflection coefficient k = (ρ₂ − ρ₁)/(ρ₁ + ρ₂) is positive when resistivity rises with depth. Mean resistivity ρ̄ = M₀·ρ₁, with M₀ from Table 5.10, gives d₁ from the measured curve. Rods reaching the lower layer use the penetration factor FK = (1+k)/(1−k+2k·d₁/L).

5.13Practical installation rulesp. 140
  • Lay counterpoises 0.5 to 1.0 m deep, below frost. Compact the backfill lightly, and keep gravel and stones away.
  • Replace corrosive soil. Install strips on edge.
  • Drive rods further apart than their length.
  • Bonds need the same conductivity and strength as the electrode, and must resist contact corrosion.
  • Keep connections short, protected and accessible. Air to soil or concrete transitions corrode most.

Where the book disagrees with itself

  • Crosswise electrode, p. 128 and 139. Table 5.8 prints ρ/(2πL) in front of the bracket. The worked example uses ρ/(8πL) to reach 8.41 Ω. With 2π the result would be 33.6 Ω, against 11.4 Ω measured, so 8π appears right. The calculators use 8π.
  • Reflection coefficient sign, p. 139. The example prints k = 0.73, but 520 over 80 Ω·m gives −0.73 by the book's own formula.
  • Largest difference, p. 134. The text defines it with RE max, but Table 5.9's 25.7 % for tower B comes from the lowest value, 7.5 Ω. Using RE max gives 18.8 %, which would pass the 20 % test. The calculator takes the largest difference either way, as the table does.
