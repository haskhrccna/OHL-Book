The chapter in 60 seconds

A line takes many years to get from idea to energising, so planning starts long before construction. Planners forecast demand, list alternatives, screen them with electrical studies, then pick the cheapest one over its life that still meets the criteria, chiefly (n−1). The result fixes voltage, conductors, circuits, tower type and AC or DC. Overhead lines remain the economic default; cables cost on average 20 times more per unit of power at the highest voltages.

Why it matters

Each decision here locks in the next. Voltage sets capacity, capacity sets conductors, conductors are 30 to 35 % of the line's cost, and the tower carries all of it for decades. An early mistake costs far more to fix than it did to avoid.

When is a new or uprated line needed? Where, and at what quality and rating? What type: overhead or cable, AC or DC? What voltage and how many circuits?

1.3Planning methodsp. 3–4

Planning narrows many options to one. Sites take three to ten or more years to obtain, which is why decisions rest on long-term load forecasts.

The final choice is not purely numerical. Utility experience, local standards and availability of spare parts also count.

1.4Planning criteria and (n−1)p. 4–5

The basic rule: no load may be lost after a single contingency. Under the (n−1) criterion the system, at heavy and at light load, must survive the outage of any one component, one at a time.

1.5Evolution and selection of voltagep. 5–8

Line capability grows with the square of voltage, which is why voltages kept climbing. Select a milestone.

EHV choices also weigh interconnection, reliability and higher short-circuit current. Series capacitors, static compensators, FACTS and high surge impedance load lines can change the answer, and so can HVDC.

1.8Direct current transmissionp. 12–14

With UDC = √2 · UAC and equal current, two DC poles carry about the same power as three AC phases. That saves a third of the conductor.

For DC

  • Lower power losses: ohmic losses 25 to 35 % lower
  • Lower switching overvoltages, so smaller tower clearances
  • No stability issue: systems need not be in synchronism
  • Links different frequencies back to back
  • Adds little to neighbouring short-circuit levels

Against DC

  • Needs expensive converter stations at both ends
  • Cannot economically be tapped by intermediate substations
  • Converters need reactive power up to 60 % of active power
  • Pollution makes insulation harder: creepage path about 30 % longer in the example

Practice: a DC line costs around 70 % of the equivalent AC line, but only long routes offset the converters. DC is also often chosen for underwater crossings. Guyed towers are usually preferred for ±600 kV (Itaipu) because they cost less.

1.9Higher order phase linesp. 14–16

More phases can raise power density in an existing corridor. Six and twelve phases are the favoured options. Phase-to-ground voltage becomes the reference, because adjacent phases sit closer in voltage.

The catch

Every terminal needs six-pole (or more) transformers, breakers and bus bars, which is costly and makes protection difficult when tied into three-phase systems. Flashover between phases also becomes more likely. Use is limited to a few lines.

1.10Investmentsp. 16–17

Voltage, circuits and conductor section matter most, followed by tower height, route, the ratio of suspension to strain towers, wind and ice loads, and required reliability. Indemnities have in some cases approached the cost of the line itself.

1.11Licences and permitsp. 17–18

Almost every country requires an environmental impact study. Managing the permit process well is vital, and that means involving people early.

What the process covers

  • Project justification
  • EMF effects
  • Visual impact
  • Interests of communities
  • Effects on land use and property value

Practice

  • Inform landowners early, including compensation and route criteria
  • Explain access roads for construction and maintenance
  • Use qualitative visual assessment with the public
  • Present designs with photomontages
  • Avoid sensitive areas; keep access roads on natural contours
1.12Underground versus overheadp. 18–21

Cables suit places where lines are not approved or not feasible: urban areas, airport approaches, substation exits, water crossings. Submarine cable may be the only option between systems separated by sea.
