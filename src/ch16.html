The chapter in 60 seconds

Building a line means many small sites along many kilometres. Foundations come first, chosen for soil and access, with concrete quality controlled by water-cement ratio. Stubs are set so that a tower will stand true once conductors load it, then towers go up by elevation, crane, gin pole or helicopter. Conductors are strung under tension over blocks and never touch the ground. Sags are set to allow for creep, and clamps are placed with an offset so that insulator sets hang plumb after clipping-in.

Why it matters

A design only works if it is built as drawn. Wrong sags break clearances, poor concrete weakens foundations, and induced voltages on a conductor being strung can kill. In long sections on steep ground, clamp offsets can reach several metres.

16.1Construction planningp. 621–624

Investigate terrain, soil, buildings, crossings and access first. Quantities and progress then give a time schedule, or a network diagram on larger projects, which sets staff, equipment and material delivery. Plotting actual progress against the plan shows slippage at any time.

Order of work

Protect the site: use biologically recyclable hydraulic oils, break up compaction, remove temporary roads, and leave no bolts, wire ends or packing, which can damage machines or harm cattle. Keep the stockyard near the route with good road links, and have material on hand before installation starts. Never drag tower members, and keep joints clean.

16.2Transportation and accessp. 624–625

Four-wheel-drive lorries, Unimogs and tractors do most of the work. Tracked vehicles and low-pressure tyres serve peat and sand, while ropeways or costly, weather-sensitive helicopters serve high mountains.

16.3.1–6Building foundationsp. 625–631

Soil and method

Secure pits against collapse. Lay 50 to 100 mm of lean concrete under reinforcement. Backfill in layers of about 0.20 m, with no roots or waste, and aim for soil close to its natural state. Finish backfill the same day or scarify before continuing, and do not compact above concrete for 24 hours.

A pile driving report

Grouting factor

Piling rigs should be light, allow rakes to 250 mm/m and drive piles of 14 to 20 m, with a hammer about as heavy as the pile. Grout is pressed in at about 10 bar. Cut-off piles need an uplift test.

Proof testing

Proof-test at least 5 % of piles. Test bare steel piles no earlier than three weeks after driving, because friction grows. Grillages need sand bedding, hand backfill and 0.30 m of crown above ground. Rock anchors use boreholes 40 mm wider than the bar, with one anchor tested per foundation.

16.3.7Concretep. 631–640

Prefer ready-mixed. Use C20/25, or at most C30/37, where 20 is the cylinder strength and 25 the 150 mm cube strength in N/mm². Site mixes should stay at C20/25. Cement should be under three months old. Suitability tests for C20/25 must show 35 N/mm² on the cube.

16.4Earthingp. 640–641

Radial or ring electrodes lie 0.6 to 1.0 m deep, with straps on edge so soil touches both sides. Avoid stones and coarse gravel at the electrode. Rods can be driven from the pit before concreting. Measure with an earth tester, and add electrodes if the value is too high. Connections must be bright metal.

16.5Setting stubs and tower inclinationp. 641–645

Set stubs with a template or tower base, or one by one on wide towers with uneven legs. Level difference should not exceed about 6 mm. Templates may come off after two days, without vibrating the legs.

Angle and dead-end towers lean under permanent conductor pull, so they are erected leaning the opposite way, at everyday load (10 °C, no wind, in EN 50341-3-4). Two aims are possible: top over centre, or longest crossarm level.

16.6Erecting supportsp. 645–653

Choosing a method

Methods

Bolt torque

Guyed towers are plumbed with two theodolites. Guy tension is 7 to 10 % of rope RTS, and the tolerance for lean and twist is 3 mm per metre of height. Cranes suit flat land, whereas hills favour manual erection or an auxiliary mast.

16.7Insulator sets and jointsp. 653

Vertical pins go in from the top so they cannot fall out. Use cradles for multiple sets. After sagging, sets may be out of plumb by at most 2 % of string length in either direction. Joints stay at least 10 m from the nearest structure, one per span and conductor, and subconductor joints at least 1.5 m apart. They may be banned in crossing spans.

16.8.1–3Stringing methods and equipmentp. 653–660

Conductors run under tension over blocks, clear of ground and obstacles, from tensioner to puller. A pilot rope goes first, then the pulling rope, then the conductor. Length under tension should not exceed 8 to 10 km. Long straight lines string over strain towers to use full reels, while dense regions string section by section.

16.8.4Stringing, sagging and earthingp. 660–666

Protect crossings with scaffolds, strong enough for a conductor failure over roads and railways. Anchor crossarms next to tensioner and puller. Place the reel stand at least six reel widths behind the tensioner. Earth all machines and bond the operators' standing plates.

16.8.5Initial sags, creep and offsetsp. 666–673

On blocks, tension is equal either side, so tension S matches, not H. Horizontal forces differ wherever heights or spans differ. In clamps, sets hang plumb only if H is equal. So stringing Hi must be chosen so that total length matches the clipped condition. Then clamps are set off by vi, and no end correction is needed.

The book's mountain section

Creep is permanent stretch from settling of strands and metal flow, and it grows with stress and temperature. Sag allowance uses the 30-year value at everyday stress, less what has already happened by sagging day. It is applied as a colder installation temperature.
