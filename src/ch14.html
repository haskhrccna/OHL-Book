The chapter in 60 seconds

A hanging conductor follows a catenary, which a parabola matches closely up to about 500 m. Tension falls when it warms and rises under ice, and the state change equation links any two conditions. Sections of several spans behave like one ruling span. Each design condition then gives a sag, and the worst sag is compared with the clearance to ground, roads or other lines. Computers do this exactly today, and knowing the hand method tells you what the program should say.

Why it matters

Sag decides tower height and clearance safety. Tension decides tower and foundation loads. A 1 m error in a crossing is a safety issue, and the book's own example shows simple methods can miss this by that much.

14.1Basisp. 540

Between supports, the conductor has constant properties. Bending stiffness is low, so tension acts alone, and the ends are pivots with no moment. Sag f is the vertical distance from the conductor to the chord between attachment points.

14.2The catenaryp. 540–544

Horizontal force H is constant along the span. With c = H/(m·g), the curve is y = c·cosh(x/c). Tension at any point is S = H·cosh(x/c), highest at the supports. Mean tension is H·L/a.

Catenary or parabola

14.3The parabolap. 544–546

Neglecting slope spreads weight along the span instead of the curve: fmax = m·g·a²/(8H), and at any point f = 4·fmax·(ξ/a)·(1 − ξ/a). Sag at a given ξ does not depend on the height difference. Length L = a·[1 + (8/3)(f/a)²].

14.5State change equationp. 546–549

Length change between two states comes from elastic stretch and thermal expansion. For a section with several spans, use the equivalent or ruling span, aid = √(Σa³/Σa). It assumes equal tension in all spans, which is better than treating each span as fixed.

14.6Concentrated loadsp. 549–551

Sag equals the bending moment of an equivalent simple beam over H. Markers or spacers count as point loads. For a load at midspan, the shear integral is G²a/4 + G·w·a²/4 + w²a³/12.

14.7Short spans with tension setsp. 551–553

In short spans, heavy tension sets cannot be ignored. Rigid sets (one long rod) use Eq. 14.53 and 14.54. Flexible sets (cap-and-pin or several rods) act as a heavy length of conductor: Eq. 14.55 and 14.56. Tension results are almost equal, and flexible sets show about 0.1 m more sag.

14.8Forces in a tensioning sectionp. 553–563

Suspension sets swing along the line, so a change in one span shifts all others. Ice in one span pulls the sets towards it: tension rises less than with fixed points, but sag rises more. Each set settles where tension difference balances its vertical load. Programs solve this system for all spans.

Inverted V sets cut this swing. With a 60° opening, a 110 kV example gained 2.42 m clearance under local ice, but tension difference rose from 1.3 to 4.0 kN. The strings must resist compression, and vibration protection is needed.
