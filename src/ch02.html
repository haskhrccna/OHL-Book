The chapter in 60 seconds

A line is an electrical component before it is a structure. Its surge impedance sets its natural power, and length decides whether heat, voltage drop or stability limits it. Voltage causes fields, corona, radio noise and audible noise. Overvoltages come in three kinds, power frequency, slow-front and fast-front, and each sets air clearances through a different formula. Wind swing then decides how much of that clearance survives. The tower head is the result of combining all of these.

Why it matters

Tower dimensions, conductor bundles and right-of-way width all come from this chapter. A bigger bundle costs money up front but can cut audible noise by about 18 dB(A), and a clearance formula decides whether a tower head is 5 m or 6.5 m wide.

2.1Lines as components of electric systemsp. 28–32

When the energy stored in the electric and magnetic fields is equal, U/I = √(L/C). That ratio is the surge impedance Zc. Power flowing at this condition is the surge impedance load or natural power: the line's reactive losses are exactly offset by its capacitance. Well above it you may need shunt capacitors, and well below it shunt reactors.

What limits a line (2.1.4)

Stability limit, Eq. 2.7

Stability. Steady-state stability concerns small disturbances and depends on the operating state. Transient stability concerns big ones such as line faults, and depends on their size and location. Voltage regulation of 5 to 10 % is the highest practical limit, and matters on radial lines up to 150 kV.

Reliability terms

  • Mechanical unavailability is the inverse of twice the design return period. All other components together add at most 25 % of that.
  • 30 to 35 % of reclosing operations after lightning fail, each costing 2 to 4 hours.
  • Switching flashover probability is low, around 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁴.
  • Power frequency causes add about 0.3 outages a year, under 10 hours each.

Compensation

  • Series capacitors offset line reactance, shortening the line electrically. Watch sub-synchronous resonance.
  • Shunt reactors absorb charging power at light load (Ferranti effect).
  • Shunt capacitors support voltage at high load.
  • Synchronous condensers and static compensators do both.

2.3Fields, corona and noisep. 33–43

Field strength E is in kV/m. Flux density B = μ₀·H, so 1 A/m = 1.256 μT in air. A 1 kV/m field drives about 0.015 mA through the body. Up to 1 mA/m² no biological effect is detected. Above 10 eye flickering can occur, 100 is the hazard threshold, and harm is expected from 1000.

What a line produces against the precaution values

Corona starts when the surface gradient reaches a critical value: about 27 kVrms/cm for a smooth 10 mm cylinder, and 21 for a stranded conductor. The book recommends staying around 17 kVrms/cm. Corona causes radio interference, audible noise and losses.

Conductor gradient, radio noise and audible noise

Radio interference

Measured at 1 MHz in dB above 1 μV/m, usually at the right-of-way edge. Signal-to-noise matters more than noise alone. Example: class C needs 22 dB, so a 66 dB signal allows 66 − 22 = 44 dB from the line. If one phase is 3 dB or more above the others, the others can be ignored. Otherwise average the top two and add 1.5 dB.

Audible noise

Mainly a foul weather effect. Residential 45 dB(A) is hard to meet permanently. Complaints are avoided under 50 dB(A) and expected above 55 dB(A), which can occur near 420 kV lines with high gradients.

2.4Line performance and insulationp. 43–50

Overvoltages are rare and brief. Power frequency voltage is low but permanent, and it governs the insulator string design through pollution and creepage distance. A clearance has to cope with each kind of stress in a different state of the line.

Three stresses, three design cases

Standard impulse shapes

Deterministic method

Assumes a definite maximum overvoltage Umax and minimum withstand Uw, with a safety margin between them. Conservative, because both extremes rarely coincide. The withstand is the BSIL for switching, BIL for lightning.

Statistical method

Treats stress and strength as distributions. Their overlap is the probability of flashover. Usual PFO is 10⁻² to 10⁻⁴, one outage in 100 to 10 000 switching operations. Air may flash over because it recovers; solid insulation may not.

Flashover risk from overlapping distributions

Air density. U50%real = U50%·(δ/khm)ⁿ, where δ = (288/T)·exp(−0.00012·h). The bracket with its exponent is the relative insulation strength. At 1000 m, 293 K, 4 m gap and 15 g/m³ the book gets 0.95.

Lightning reaches phases by shielding failure, past the earth wire, or by back-flashover, when stroke current raises the tower potential. 60 to 70 % of lightning flashovers are transient and reclose. The measure is outages per 100 km per year. Live-line work can also govern clearances.

Del and Dpp are the basic air distances. Internal clearances can be reduced during rare events, because a flashover there only interrupts supply. External clearances protect people, so Del always applies and a Dadd is added, with Cmin ≥ 1.1·asom to ensure any flashover stays on the line.

Wind statistics

  • Extreme yearly winds follow a Gumbel distribution, Eq. 2.57.
  • Time within a year follows a Weibull, Eq. 2.58, β = 2.
  • Only sustained 5 to 10 minute averages move a conductor, not gusts.
  • Scatter of swing: σ = 2.25·[1 − exp(−V²/230)]°.
  • Only wind perpendicular to the line gives full swing, so probability is halved above about 2°.

Swing angle and tower head check

Midspan spacing, Eq. 2.68

Three design routes in the book. The statistical route gave 6.5 m phase spacing at 380 kV, the European standard route 5.8 m. Brazilian compact practice checks only power frequency at extreme swing, with phases asynchronous by about 8°, and gets 2.56 m in a 5.5 m spaced 500 kV line where Eq. 2.68 would demand 6.27 m.

Where the book disagrees with itself

These are places where a printed number or statement conflicts with the book's own formula or table. The calculators here follow the formulas.

  • Table 2.7, p. 38. k₂ for 8 subconductors is printed 6.07, but the formula shown gives 5.36. For 3 it is 3.48 against 3.46.
  • Bundle circle radius, p. 38. The text gives r₀ = s/sin(π/n₂). Chapter 3 gives the correct s/[2·sin(π/n₂)], which is what Table 2.7 uses.
  • Subconductor spacing, p. 42. The text says more spacing reduces radio and audible noise and raises fields. Table 2.10 and the same paragraph's end say the effect is minor, with only audible noise rising. The text probably means phase spacing.
  • Table 2.29, p. 72. Eq. 2.57 reproduces most columns, but gives 25.2 m/s at 20 years where 26.5 is printed, and 29.7 at 150 years where 30.9 is printed.
  • Swing angle, p. 72. Eq. 2.63 with the listed data gives about 48.5°, about a degree above the printed 47.6°. The book's bracket uses the span, 400 m, although Table 2.28 lists 500 m as wind span.
