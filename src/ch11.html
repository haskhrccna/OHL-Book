The chapter in 60 seconds

Wind makes conductors move in three main ways. Steady light wind sheds vortices and drives small, fast aeolian vibration, which fatigues strands at the clamps. Bundles behind one another in moderate wind get subspan oscillation. Ice plus strong wind gives galloping, with huge slow loops that can clash phases. Tension is the key lever for aeolian vibration: higher tension raises vibration and cuts self-damping. Dampers remove the energy. Field recorders and Miner's rule then estimate the conductor's life.

Why it matters

An hour of vibration a day adds 10⁷ to 10⁸ cycles a year. Metal fatigue at the clamp then breaks strands far below static strength. Damping systems cost little against that risk.

Frequency and wind ranges

11.2Aeolian vibrationsp. 323–342

A span behaves like a string. Bending stiffness is small, so natural frequencies are fk = (k/2a)·√(H/mc). The spectrum is so dense that vortex shedding always finds a match. Near resonance, vortices lock on to the conductor frequency.

Wind, frequency and standing waves

Energy balance. In steady vibration, wind power in equals power lost in self-damping plus dampers: PW = PS + PD. Free-span angle ψ = 2π·A/λ is the intensity measure. Laminar air over flat land, water, snow, sand, or at great heights makes it worse; boulders, trees and buildings break it up. Light conductors reach steady vibration faster.

Life from a field recording

Fatigue test results give about 22 N/mm² at 5·10⁸ cycles, or about 300 μm/m. The usual design limit is half: 150 μm/m. Single-layer ACSR endurance is about 22.5 N/mm². Fretting, where strands rub, starts cracks. Creep moves load to steel and improves fatigue life in composite conductors.

Damping systems

Verifying. Mobile laser stands measure from 30 to 150 m without contact. A recorder fitted to the clamp measures 89 mm from last contact, for 10 s every 15 minutes, for about 3 to 4 months, in a 16 × 16 amplitude-frequency matrix.

11.3Subspan oscillationsp. 341–342

Leeward subconductors in the wake of windward ones get unstable. Horizontal twins are most affected, worse if twisted by 5 to 15°. Oscillation fatigues conductors near spacers, and icing makes it severe.

Bundle check

11.4Gallopingp. 342–344

Ice gives the conductor a lifting shape. Uplift and twist vary together, feeding a large vertical ellipse, with long axis to short axis from 6:1 to 2:1. Amplitudes of up to 12 m have been seen. Flashovers, melted conductors, damaged fittings and towers follow.

Remedies

11.5Short-circuit oscillationsp. 344–345

In single conductor lines, short-circuit forces are negligible, because phases are far apart and natural frequency is below 0.3 Hz. In bundles, subconductors attract strongly and clash, deforming the outer layer and causing noise and fatigue. Spacers must resist this without permanent deformation. Only subspans of 5 to 10 m would avoid clashing, which is practical in substations only. Fast clearing can avoid it even at high currents.
