The chapter in 60 seconds

Conductors carry the power and take 30 to 50 % of line investment. Most are aluminium stranded over steel: aluminium for conduction, steel for strength, with creep moving load onto the steel over time. A conductor is chosen by checking four things: current it can carry without overheating, cost of losses over its life, surface gradient at the working voltage, and mechanical stress in everyday and extreme conditions. Bundles beat one large conductor on nearly all counts at high voltage.

Why it matters

The conductor is the line's largest single purchase, and losses go on costing for decades. It also sets tower loads and sags. A choice made only on current often turns out to be wrong economically.

Why composite works

Aluminium creeps, steel hardly does. Over time the aluminium stress falls and steel stress rises, which improves resistance to vibration fatigue. Mono-metallic conductors cannot shift load this way and are more prone to fatigue. Grease the steel core: at minimum case 1, core only. Do not grease the outer layer, as it can cause corona and affect clamps.

Stress-strain test

Sample at least 400 × diameter and 10 m. Load in steps to 30, 50, 70 and 85 % RTS, holding each, then to failure. Failure at 95 % RTS or above passes. Creep follows ε = ε1h·tⁿ, a straight line on log-log axes, extrapolated to 100 000 hours.

Creep over time

Bundles. Corona at 220 kV in 1929 drove the move to larger diameters and then to bundles, patented in 1930. Lines at 420 kV and above are not feasible without them. Usual spacing is 400 mm in Europe, 457 mm in North America, and over 18 × subconductor diameter to counter subspan oscillation.

Fixed ratings use cautious weather. In reality the wind exceeds 1 m/s over 98 % of the time, so weather-based forecasts can raise capacity by 25 to 30 %. This suits exceptional operation only, as continuous running at the limit wastes energy.

Gradients vary by phase. In the book's quad example they are 12.6, 15.4 and 16.0 kV/cm. Corona losses matter above 300 kV: about 2 kW/km fair, 5 rainy, up to 100 frosty. They do not affect conductor selection, and do not peak with Joule losses because foul weather cools conductors.

7.4Mechanical designp. 235–239

Limits work at two ends. Extreme loads: tension may not exceed RTS/1.25. EN 50341-3-4 also multiplies worst case stress by 1.30 and compares with long-term stress. Everyday stress: keeps vibration fatigue away. It often sets stringing tension at 110 kV and above.

Stress limits, Table 7.17

Conductors with a small steel share, mono-metallic ones, over 25 mm diameter or in spans above 500 m are more prone to fatigue. A higher everyday stress lowers towers, but raises loads on angle towers. At 20 % more EDS on a 220 kV line, investment fell 4 % with 10 % strain towers, and stayed level with 20 %. The Elbe crossing optimum was near 70 N/mm². Allow for creep with lower initial sags.
