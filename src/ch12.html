The chapter in 60 seconds

Supports hold conductors at safe clearances and carry their loads to the ground. Their function in the line sets the type: suspension, angle, strain or dead-end. Their head comes from clearances, their strength from load cases multiplied by partial factors. Most high-voltage supports are lattice steel: angle sections in axial force, where compression buckling and bolted connections govern. Poles of steel, concrete or wood use bending checks and deflection limits. Prototype tests prove the design.

Why it matters

Supports set most of what the public sees and a major share of line cost. Design is iterative: tower weight feeds wind load, which feeds member sizes. Understanding which load case governs which member is the core skill.

12.5Lattice steel towersp. 374–441

Members and joints

  • Hot-rolled angles, up to L250 × 28, 133 cm².
  • Cruciform or compound sections beyond that.
  • Minimum thickness 4 mm, bolts M12, smallest L35 × 4.
  • No more than six bolts in a line.
  • Punching full size only to 12 mm in EN 50341-3-4, and not on permanently tensioned crossarm members.
  • End distance 1.2·d₀, edge 1.5·d₀, pitch 2.2·d₀.

Steel and corrosion

  • S235: fy 235, fu 360 N/mm². S355: 355, 510.
  • Bolts 4.6, 5.6, 8.8 and 10.9. Avoid 5.8 and 6.8 for low ductility.
  • Hot-dip galvanising loses 2 to 10 μm a year, so 10 to 15 years before more protection.
  • Silicon sets zinc thickness: S355 often looks dull grey.
  • Duplex coating, about 70 μm, more than doubles life.

Member forces come from a plane truss by sections, or a three-dimensional model. Y and portal towers need the latter: about 400 nodes and 600 members, so input errors are a real hazard.

Compression member check

Staggered bracing uses 1.2·L/iyy. Thin angles at low slenderness can fail in flexural torsional buckling, λvi ≈ 5·b/t, or the better Eq. 12.80. Bracings on one leg suffer eccentricity, cancelled by end restraint at λ̄ = √2.

Tower top deflection

Upgrading. Swap bracings. Add redundants if flexural buckling governs and tension is sufficient. Add angles via batten plates to form cruciform legs, using fitted bolts rather than welding, with at least one third of buckling length spacing and 2.5 % shear force. The existing leg stays loaded while the new angle is added, so it carries more than its share.

12.6Steel polesp. 442–448

Polygonal 8, 12 or 16-sided cones, in lengths to about 15 m, joined by slip joints (overlap 1.5 × diameter, slope at least 10 mm/m, wall to 16 mm) or bolted flanges. Second order moments are needed: MII = 1.05·MI or 1.03·MI as a German shortcut. Thick coatings last 20 years.

12.8Wood polesp. 459–461

Spans up to about 50 m with single poles, up to 250 m with portals, H-frames up to 345 kV. Decay fastest at the ground line, so high safety factors of 4 to 10 apply and poles are treated with creosote or similar, for 30 years at least. Double poles count three times the modulus in their plane, twice otherwise.

Where the book disagrees with itself

  • Table reference, p. 362. The text cites Table 2.21 for kc. It is Table 2.22, as the example below it says.
  • Mountable members. Here members up to 30° from horizontal must carry 1.0 kN, p. 416. In Chapter 6, EN 50341-1 is quoted as under 60°, p. 186. These cannot both describe the same rule.
  • Vibrator frequency, p. 453. "15000 Hz" for vibrators appears implausibly high and may mean revolutions per minute.
  • Standard number, p. 370. Table 12.1's caption prints EN 50431-1 for EN 50341-1.
