The chapter in 60 seconds

Every network study treats a line as a few numbers per kilometre: resistance, reactance and capacitance, in positive and zero sequence. Positive sequence is normal balanced operation. Zero sequence is what a phase-to-ground fault sees, with current returning through earth and earth wires. Short lines need only series impedance, medium lines a nominal Π-circuit, and long lines the exact hyperbolic equations or an equivalent Π-circuit that gives the same answer.

Why it matters

These parameters feed load flows, losses, short-circuit and stability studies, relay settings and compensation design. A wrong zero-sequence value means a wrong fault current and a wrong protection setting.

DC resistance per unit length is ρ/A. AC resistance can be higher through skin and spiral effects (Eq. 7.16). A bundle's resistance is that of one subconductor divided by n₂. Annealed copper at 58 m/(Ω·mm²) is the 100 % IACS reference; hard-drawn copper is about 97 %, hard-drawn aluminium 61 %.

3.3Positive-sequence impedancep. 82–85

Z₁' = R₁' + jX₁'. Reactance depends only logarithmically on geometry, so X₁' changes little across voltage levels unless bundling raises the equivalent radius. A fully transposed line has each phase order for one third of its length.

3.4Zero-sequence impedancep. 85–88

Zero-sequence currents are equal and in phase, so they must return through earth or earth wires. The field differs greatly, and X₀ is 1.5 to 4 times X₁. Its main use is phase-to-ground fault current and ground relay settings. Simplified formulae derive from Pollaczek and Carson.

δ = 1.85 / √(ω·μ₀/ρE)   return depth of earth current (3.15)

What earth wires do to Z₀

Relative permeability: about 1 for copper, aluminium and ACSR with Al:St ratio of 6 or more, 5 to 10 for ACSR with one aluminium layer, and about 25 for steel earth wires.

3.5Capacitance and capacitive reactancep. 88–91

Conductors, earth wires and ground form a capacitance network. Xc = 1/(2πfC). Converting the delta of phase-to-phase capacitances C to a star gives C₁ = C₀ + 3C (3.29).

3.6Admittancep. 91

Y' = G' + jB'. For overhead lines the conductance G' is very small and neglected, so Y' ≈ jB' = 1/Xc. The book's line: 2π·50·12.0 nF/km = 3.77 μS/km positive sequence and 2.87 μS/km zero sequence.

How wrong is the nominal Π-circuit?

Wave length is λ = 2π/β, about 5870 km at 50 Hz and 4895 km at 60 Hz. Velocity is f·λ. A line terminated in Zc has no reflected wave: a flat or infinite line. The angle of Zc is usually between 0 and −15°.

Where the book disagrees with itself

  • Same line, three answers, p. 95 to 97. The exponential solution gives 519.5 kV, the hyperbolic one 520.3 kV, and the power line then uses 520.5 kV. An unrounded solution gives 519.7 kV, 1026 MW sending power and 26 MW losses, where the book prints 1028 and 28 MW. The differences come from rounding. The solver here uses unrounded values.
  • Phase constant, p. 95. β is printed as 1.042·10⁻³, but 1.073·10⁻³ at 87.95° gives 1.072·10⁻³, and the 5861 km wave length only follows from that value.
  • Equation reference, p. 96. "Equation (3.59) yields" Us, but the calculation shown is (3.57).
  • Equivalent series resistance, p. 97. Table 3.3 lists 6.45 Ω. Unrounded values give 6.32 Ω; 90.15∠85.9° is sensitive to the rounded angle.
