The chapter in 60 seconds

Insulators carry the conductor and keep the voltage off the tower, year after year, in rain, salt and dust. Three materials compete: porcelain, toughened glass and composite. Two forms dominate: cap-and-pin strings and long rods. Mechanical load sets the rating, overvoltages set the arcing distance, and pollution sets creepage distance, which often governs the number of units. Most of the operating risk comes from pollution layers that turn conductive when moistened.

Why it matters

Insulators are a small share of cost, yet a poor choice brings repeated flashovers. In the Cigré study, mean time between failures for 10 000 insulator sets ranged from 10 hours to 18 years depending on type.

9.2Ceramic insulatorsp. 257–264

From bell to long rod

Modern types are C-120 and high-strength C-130 aluminous porcelain. Glazing reduces dirt and adds prestress. Strength falls as cross section grows, so test bar values do not apply directly to a large body. Production: mix, extrude, dry, shape on lathes, glaze, fire. Fittings are set with lead antimony alloy or Portland cement.

Insulating crossarms replace steel, shrinking width and height, up to 145 kV in some countries, and suit uprating.

9.3Glass insulatorsp. 265–267

Heated to about 600 °C and chilled with air, the surface compresses and the core stays in tension. This toughening raises strength but makes the shell shatter on impact, which is easy to see from the ground. Broken shells still hold about 90 % of the pin force. Solid-core puncture-proof glass cannot be made, so glass is cap-and-pin only. Failures stop after the first year, at about 4·10⁻⁵ a year. Zinc sleeves protect pins on DC or in pollution.

9.4Composite insulatorsp. 267–269

A glass fibre reinforced rod carries load. Silicone rubber sheds keep water off and give creepage. The rod uses ECR glass against brittle failure, with crimped fittings. The critical weakness is the seal between fitting, rod and housing: humidity entering can cause brittle fracture.

Low weight pays off most at 500 kV and above. The water-repellent surface suits pollution, coasts, DC, and uprating, since one piece can replace a longer set without changing the tower. They are also used as phase spacers against galloping.

9.7Design of insulator setsp. 279–281

Double suspension sets are common in populated areas and at crossings. In line with the conductor, they resist arc damage better, do not form a parallelogram when swinging, and are less visible. Across the line, a broken string shocks the survivor less. Arcing horns or rings guide power arcs, up to 12 000 K, away from the insulators. Above 220 kV they also control the field. Tension sets are double or more, on yoke plates that limit impact after a string fails.

DC lines. Operating voltage governs under all pollution levels. Particles are drawn in by the unidirectional field, so pollution is 10 to 100 times higher than on AC. Use long creepage, about 30 mm/kV at light pollution, with 5 % above rated pole voltage. Anti-fog cap-and-pin with creepage to height of 2.8 to 3.2, zinc sleeves, grading rings, silicone grease or composite insulators all help.

9.9Performance in servicep. 287–294

Voltage along a string

With conductive layers, flashover can drop to about 20 % of the dry, clean value, so operating voltage alone may flash it over. Surface current losses are only about one thousandth of total losses.

Keeping insulation capable

Dead-end sets clean better, so some utilities reduce creepage 10 %. Suspension insulators also protect large birds from flashovers. Lines at 90 kV and above use suspension insulators only on straight sections.

Where the book disagrees with itself

  • Insulation level name, p. 283. The text uses BSIL for fast-front overvoltages. Chapter 2 calls that BIL, and BSIL the switching level.
  • Table reference in Chapter 2. L 100 C 325 and L 210 C 550 are cited to Table 9.4. They are in Table 9.2 and 9.3 here; Table 9.4 lists ceramic materials.
  • Material factor, p. 298. Uses a material factor of 2.3, while the mechanical design text says 2.0 under EN 50341-1. Both are right in context, since 2.3 is the German national value, but the book does not state this at the point of use.
